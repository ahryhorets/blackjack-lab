Part 1 · How the casino wins

the house's playbook

1. One unfair rule is enough

Everything in blackjack is symmetric except one thing: you act first. If you bust, you lose, even if the dealer busts afterwards. That single rule is the house edge. Every "extra" you get (3:2 blackjacks, doubling, splitting, surrender) only exists to win some of it back, and only if you use them at the right moments.

"Never bust" sounds careful. It loses about 37× more than the chart, because standing on 12–16 against a 7–A loses more than half the time. The casino doesn't need to cheat: intuition does the work.

6:5 blackjack is the big one. Blackjacks arrive about 4.75% of the time; paying 1.2 instead of 1.5 takes 0.3 bets each time. It looks like a tiny change on the felt and it multiplies the house edge several times. Smaller tweaks (H17, no surrender) are within the simulation's noise at 500k hands, which is itself a lesson: small edges need huge samples to see.

4. Insurance: the side bet that sounds like protection

Insurance pays 2:1 if the dealer's hidden card is a ten. Tens left with an Ace showing: 96 of 311 cards = 30.9% EV = 2 × p − 1 × (1 − p) = 3p − 1 = -0.074 → you lose ~7.4% of every insurance bet Simulated, always insuring: -7.08% on 76,715 staked

It only breaks even when more than 1 in 3 unseen cards are tens. Off the top of the shoe, never.

5. "Quit while you're ahead"

It feels like a winning system: most nights you go home up. But the losing nights are about 3.5× bigger, and the total is still negative. No stopping rule can change the value of the hands you do play. Mathematicians call it the optional stopping theorem; casinos call it a good night.

6. Martingale: double after every loss

On a 2,000 bankroll with 10-chip units, martingale went broke after 425 hands. Flat betting the same game lasted 121,690 hands. Seven losses in a row (about 1 in 100 streaks) needs a 1,280 bet; the table limit and your bankroll end the system. Many small wins, one huge loss: the same shape as "quit while ahead".

The playbook in one line: frequent small wins, rare big losses, fast hands, and rules nobody reads. None of it needs luck. It needs volume.

9. The only real edge: count, then size your bets

Cards that have been dealt don't come back until the shuffle, so the shoe has memory. Hi-Lo counting: 2–6 = +1, 7–9 = 0, tens and Aces = −1; divide by decks left to get the true count. Rich shoe → bet more.

Counting with a flat bet barely helps (-0.10%). Betting 1 unit in bad shoes and up to 12 units in good ones: +0.66% (95% range +0.51% to +0.82%, 4 million hands). An earlier version of this page said +0.93%: that was one 200,000-hand run, and the strategy swings so much that 200k hands can't pin it down. A reader's question made us rerun it at 20× the size (see Part 4). The edge is thin and slow:

Same edge, bigger bets → more ruin. A safe unit is about 1/1,000 of the bankroll (Kelly sizing).

Part 4 · Does the random generator matter?

A reader asked a sharp question: every number here comes from Python's default generator, the Mersenne Twister (random.Random): fast and repeatable, but not cryptographically secure. Would the results survive real OS-level randomness (random.SystemRandom, i.e. os.urandom)?

So the entire experiment suite was rerun with SystemRandom, same sizes, and every published number compared with a z-score (difference ÷ combined standard error). |z| < 2 is normal noise; with 23 comparisons, about 1 would cross 2 by pure chance even with identical generators.

23 comparisons: 23 same, 0 borderline, 0 different. Martingale, 200 runs each: median 632 vs 659 hands until broke. Counting + bet sizing, 2M hands each: +0.68% vs +0.64% (±0.22).

Verdict: the generator doesn't move any result. A shuffled shoe only needs each order to be equally likely, and both generators deliver that at this scale. SystemRandom was about 1.2× slower. The check also exposed a weaker claim of our own: counting's 200k-hand edge (+0.93%) moved to +0.14% under the other generator, still "same" statistically, because that strategy is very swingy. At 2 million hands per generator both agree on about +0.66%, which is now the headline. Lesson: a question about the method is worth more than agreement with the result.

Part 5 · How this was built

Built with AI (Claude) and directed, tested and studied by the author. It started as a side project inspired by a uni Python assignment, and is completely separate from it. Everything is checked rather than trusted: 19 unit tests, results compared against published blackjack tables, a 3,000-hand random-input fuzz run, and every experiment is seeded, so you get the same numbers.

Known simplifications: dealer odds aren't recomputed for every card you might draw; split EV ignores resplits; the count-to-edge rule (−0.5% + 0.5% per true count) is a rule of thumb; one player at the table.
